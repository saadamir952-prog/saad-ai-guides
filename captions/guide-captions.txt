WHY IT WORKS

A viewer decides in the first second whether to keep watching, and the captions are the first thing their eye tracks. A full sentence on screen is a wall — nobody reads a wall, they scroll past it. One word at a time gives the eye a rhythm to follow, and a highlighted keyword gives it somewhere to land.

The timing is the part people miss. A caption that arrives after the word was spoken feels broken — the eye and the ear disagree, and the viewer leaves without knowing why. A caption that lands on the exact frame the word is spoken feels invisible, and invisible is the point: the viewer watches the video, not the captions.

THE CAPTION CHECKLIST

Check every caption against these five rules before you post:

1. One word at a time. Never the whole sentence at once. A wall of text is a scroll signal.
2. Every word on the frame it was spoken. Not early, not late. If the caption and the voice disagree by even a few frames, it feels wrong.
3. One word to land on. Highlight the keyword in each line — the word that carries the meaning. If every word looks the same, the eye has nowhere to land.
4. Big enough to read at a glance. If the viewer has to squint, the caption has already failed. Test it on a phone, at arm's length.
5. Keyword pops, not decoration. A pop draws the eye to the word that matters. Popping every word is the same as popping none.

MISTAKE ONE — THE WHOLE SENTENCE AT ONCE

The most common caption mistake: the full sentence appears the moment the speaker starts it, and sits there until the sentence ends. The viewer reads ahead, gets bored, and scrolls — or doesn't read at all, because a wall of text looks like work.

Fix: show one word at a time, each word appearing on the frame it is spoken. The viewer's eye stays with the voice instead of racing ahead of it.

MISTAKE TWO — EVERY WORD LOOKS THE SAME

Same size, same weight, same color, every word. Nothing tells the eye where the meaning is, so the eye wanders — and a wandering eye scrolls.

Fix: pick the keyword in each line and make it the one thing that stands out — a different color, a bolder weight, a small pop as it lands. One highlighted word per line is enough. Two is a competition; three is noise.

THE CATCH

Captions can't save a boring video — they sell a good one. If the video itself has nothing worth watching, perfect captions just make the boredom legible. Make the video worth selling first; the captions are how you sell it.
